A Pre-Season Refresher for Coaches

Coaches design training plans, schedule practices, and are usually the first to notice changes in performance. That makes you a key part of closing this knowledge gap — even if you've never experienced a menstrual cycle yourself.

Why this is part of your job

The menstrual cycle can affect strength, injury risk, energy, and mood (see Athletic Impacts). If you don't know this, fluctuations in performance can look like inconsistency or lack of effort when they're actually physiological. Understanding the cycle helps you coach more accurately and fairly.

What to watch for

Energy dips

Often in the days leading up to menstruation, and sometimes during it.

Mood changes

More common in the luteal phase, tied to hormone shifts, not attitude.

Injury risk

Some research points to higher injury risk during the luteal phase — worth factoring into load management.

How to talk about it

Research (Brown et al., 2020) shows athletes are often less comfortable discussing their cycle with male coaches. You don't need every detail to support your athletes well:

  • Normalize the topic generally, rather than singling anyone out.
  • Let athletes opt in to sharing specifics — don't require it.
  • Avoid assuming a dip in performance is "just" the cycle — ask, don't assume.
  • Build flexibility into training plans so athletes can self-adjust without stigma.
  • Consider a female assistant coach or trainer as an additional point of contact, if possible.
Jones et al. (2024) found that creating a supportive environment depends on both sides: coaches noticing patterns, and athletes understanding their own cycles well enough to communicate what they need.
