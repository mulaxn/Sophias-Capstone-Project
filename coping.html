Coping & Support

In our interviews, most athletes managed symptoms by "sucking it up," taking ibuprofen, or simply pushing through. Some found that staying active actually helped with cramps. Here are some general, evidence-informed strategies — this isn't medical advice, and what works varies from person to person.

Track your cycle

Knowing roughly where you are in your cycle helps you anticipate energy dips, mood shifts, or higher injury-risk windows — instead of being caught off guard.

Adjust training load thoughtfully

Some athletes find heavier strength work feels better in the follicular phase, and ease back slightly during the luteal phase. Listen to your body rather than forcing a fixed plan.

Prioritize nutrition

Appetite and nutrient needs shift across the cycle. Don't restrict during higher-appetite phases — your body may genuinely need more fuel.

Manage pain proactively

Light movement, hydration, heat, and rest can all help with cramps for some people. If pain is severe or disrupts daily life, that's worth discussing with a doctor — it's not something you just have to accept.

Talk to your coach

You don't have to share details you're not comfortable with, but letting a coach know you're adjusting for your cycle (without needing to explain everything) can open the door to more flexible training.

Normalize the conversation

The more athletes and coaches talk about this openly, the less stigma there is — and the easier it becomes to actually use what you know.

When to see a doctor: very heavy bleeding, pain that stops you from daily activities, irregular cycles, or symptoms that are getting worse over time are all good reasons to check in with a healthcare provider.
